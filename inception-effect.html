The Inception Effect

A student can use AI without ever asking it to write — and still hand in the machine's thinking as their own. This page names that phenomenon, and shows it happening, line by line, in a real (shortened and anonymised) student chat.

What it is

When "just getting suggestions" becomes something else

The Inception Effect is what happens when a student uses AI only for "help" — feedback, suggestions, a second opinion — and, suggestion by suggestion, the AI's words and reasoning migrate into the work until the thinking on the page is the machine's, not the student's. The student never asks it to write. Often, they don't even notice it happening.

I named it after a case in one of my classes. A student was flagged by our AI detector. She came to me in tears, insisting she had only asked ChatGPT for suggestions and had written every word herself. She was telling the truth about both. So I asked to see the chat — and what it showed was not a student writing with a little help. It was a student typing out an argument the AI was quietly composing for her.

She supplied the keystrokes. The AI supplied the thinking. And the detector was right to flag it.

How AI is doing the thinking

Two things migrate — and the second one is the problem

It is tempting to see this as a vocabulary issue — a few borrowed words. It isn't. Two different things cross from the chat into her paper, and they are not equal.

The words

Her language, replaced

Where she reached for a word, the AI handed her a better one and she took it. This is the visible, obvious layer — and the smaller problem.

exalts disconnected from nature imposed
The reasoning

Her thinking, supplied

The AI typed out the entire chain of argument — "they don't value their ancestors → so not the land → so disconnected from nature → so unworthy" — and she restructured her paragraph to match it. It even praised the reasoning it had just written as "exactly the kind of reasoning an English teacher looks for."

That second layer is what makes this academic dishonesty rather than editing. A writing assessment measures a student's thinking — how they build an argument, connect evidence, and reach a judgement. Here, that work was done by the model. The logical spine of the paragraph, the "why" behind each point, the bridge between one idea and the next: all of it arrived in the chat first and was copied into the essay after. She was the hand; the model was the mind.

This is also why "it's just co-writing" doesn't hold. Yes, the paper got better, and yes, she had input — she chose to accept each suggestion. But the AI was in the driver's seat, and that distinction matters for a reason beyond honesty. Researchers at the MIT Media Lab found that people who wrote with a chatbot showed measurably lower brain engagement and frequently could not quote a sentence of the essay they had just "written." When the reasoning is outsourced, the thinking that writing is supposed to build simply doesn't happen. A stronger paper, a weaker writer.

The most unsettling part is that students in this situation are usually not lying when they say they didn't cheat. They asked for help and typed every word. They cannot see that the ideas stopped being theirs somewhere around the third suggestion — which is precisely why we have to be able to show them.

Why this matters for how we assess writing

If a student can offload the thinking without ever triggering the word "cheating" in their own mind, then good intentions won't protect academic integrity — and neither will asking students to promise they "only got help." What protects it is a baseline of supervised, known-authentic writing to compare against, and the ability to show a student, concretely, how the machine's reasoning ended up on their page. That is what this simulation is built to do.
