Is AI Detection Reliable? Here's What Our Internal Research Shows.

A plain-language summary of what we found when we tested Turnitin's AI detector — for parents, and for students — and what it means for the writing students do at home.

Background

What Is Turnitin's AI Detector?

Turnitin is the same tool schools have used for years to check for plagiarism. In recent years, Turnitin added an AI writing detector — a system trained to identify text generated by tools like ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini.

When a student submits a paper, Turnitin returns an AI percentage score. Turnitin is deliberately conservative: it only reports a score above 20% when it has high confidence that AI-generated content is present. Below 20%, it withholds a number entirely and shows "*%" — meaning it's uncertain and declines to make a call.

Why 20%? Turnitin's own research, validated against 800,000 documents written before AI tools existed, shows that above the 20% threshold, the false positive rate — flagging a student who did not use AI — drops below 1%. The tool is designed to err on the side of the student.

A common question is whether this tool can be trusted. We didn't take that on faith. We tested it ourselves.

Our Testing

We Tested It With Documents of Known Authorship

To validate Turnitin's accuracy, we gathered 44 documents with known authorship — including graduate-level academic papers, model essays, AP Research submissions from 2023, and IB Extended Essays and TOK essays written in 2022, before AI tools like ChatGPT were publicly available. We also included one document written entirely using ChatGPT.

If Turnitin is unreliable, we would expect to see authentic documents flagged incorrectly. Here is what we found:

The key point: Turnitin returns 0% on writing we know is human — old papers and this year's supervised papers alike. So the flags on at-home writing are not a tool problem. The baseline for authentic student writing at this school is 0%. The tool is the same in both rooms. What changes is whether anyone is watching.
And in between: on two other in-class assignments where I did not watch students' screens, two papers came back flagged for some AI. When I watch screens, I never get flags. When I don't, I do. Either this is a classroom application of Heisenberg's uncertainty principle — observing the student changes the result — or, when I'm not watching, something else is happening. Voluntary Disclosure Studies

We Asked Students to Come Forward — And They Did

Across two separate studies — one in AP Language, one involving IB Extended Essays — students were informed that papers had been reviewed and were invited to come forward voluntarily if they had used AI. There were no consequences for disclosure. Students did not know whether they had been flagged or not.

The results were striking in two directions: the students who came forward were overwhelmingly the ones Turnitin had flagged — and not one flagged student came forward to say the tool had wrongly accused them.

Conclusion

What This Means

The data points in one direction. Turnitin's AI detector, used at the 20% threshold, is a reliable tool. It does not flag authentic student writing — 105 human-written documents, including 62 written by current students in class, and not one was flagged. It correctly identifies AI-generated content. And when students who were flagged were given a private, consequence-free opportunity to come forward, most did — confirming what the tool had already found.

This is not about punishing students or assuming the worst. It is about ensuring that the work students submit genuinely represents their own thinking and effort — which is the foundation of any meaningful academic credential.
