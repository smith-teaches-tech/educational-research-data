Executive Summary

AI use in student academic writing at this school is widespread, increasing, and going unaddressed — not for lack of a detection tool, but for lack of institutional consequences. Turnitin is working. Our response to what it tells us is not.

Three findings drive this report. First: across 44 documents with known authorship, Turnitin returned zero false positives above the 20% threshold — and 28 pre-ChatGPT IB papers from this school, run through Turnitin's current model, all returned 0% AI. The tool works. Second: two amnesty studies (AP Language, Extended Essay) produced a 77% confession rate among flagged students. One unflagged student admitted AI use Turnitin had missed. Not one flagged student came forward to dispute their flag. Third: between September 2025 and January 2026, with no policy change in between, the IB Cohort 14 flagging rate rose 63% — from 23.5% on the EE to 38.2% on the TOK essay.

Our current response — accepting Google Docs revision history as proof of authorship — is easily circumvented and tells students there are no real consequences. The data shows the result. This report proposes a supervised-writing protocol to close the gap.

Section 01 — Validation
Turnitin Works. Here Is the Evidence.

Before drawing any conclusions from student data, I tested Turnitin against documents with known authorship — papers I wrote myself, pre-ChatGPT student work, and one essay I produced using ChatGPT paragraph-by-paragraph to mimic how students actually use it.

External research, in one line: Turnitin's false-positive rate at the 20%+ threshold is under 1% — validated against 800,000 pre-ChatGPT documents (Turnitin AIW-2, 2024) and corroborated by independent studies (Temple University; Perkins et al., 2024). Below 20%, Turnitin shows "*%" rather than a score, by design, to protect students at the margin.

Section 02 — The Amnesty Studies
When Offered a Penalty-Free Exit, the Flagged Students Walked Through It.

Across two separate courses — AP Language and IB Extended Essay — every student was offered amnesty: come forward voluntarily, no consequences, regardless of Turnitin score. The patterns were near-identical. 91 papers, 26 flagged above 20%, and the students who walked through the door were almost entirely the ones the tool had identified.

AP Language: 14 of 18 flagged students came forward (78%). Extended Essay: 6 of 8 flagged students came forward (75%). One unflagged student admitted AI use Turnitin had missed. Not one flagged student came forward to dispute the flag. Six flagged students stayed silent — they may be refusing to admit use, or some may be genuine false positives. A supervised writing comparison is the only way to resolve that.

A Critical Finding Inside the Confession Data

Even the Students Who Confessed Were Under-Reporting.

All 6 EE students who came forward described their AI use as minimal and sanctioned — brainstorming, grammar checking, outlining. Investigation of at least two of them showed something different: AI had been used to rewrite full paragraphs and "tighten" prose — substantive authorship, not peripheral support. Self-reporting alone cannot serve as the basis for an integrity determination. Students either consciously minimize, or genuinely cannot recognize how much AI shaped their writing. This is precisely why a supervised writing comparison is essential.

Section 03 — A Qualitative Finding
The "Inception Effect": AI Influence Students Don't Recognize
🧠

When Students Don't Know They're Being Influenced

Two AP Language students who came forward during amnesty claimed they had only used ChatGPT for feedback — not to rewrite their essays. The teacher reviewed their actual ChatGPT logs. What the logs showed: ChatGPT kept rewriting their ideas in small snippets, even when the students asked only for feedback. Those fragments — rephrased analysis, restructured sentences, sharpened claims — migrated into the paper as the students continued working. The students had no awareness their writing was being shaped by AI. Turnitin flagged it anyway.

What Students Said
"I only asked it for feedback, not to write for me."
What the Logs Showed
ChatGPT rewrote ideas incrementally; students incorporated the fragments without realizing.
What Turnitin Found
Flagged. Even unintentional AI influence registers in the statistical patterns the tool detects.
Policy implication: Students are not always consciously cheating. AI tools are designed to be persuasive and helpful — and that helpfulness becomes authorship contamination. Our policies must address not just intentional misuse, but the structural temptation that unsupervised AI access creates.

The Inconclusive Zone

Beyond the 21 flagged Cohort 14 papers, 20 more (8 EE + 12 TOK) returned an inconclusive "*%" — Turnitin detected an AI signal but withheld a score. These are not cleared. They are the borderline cases the tool deliberately doesn't accuse.

Repeat Offenders

6 students were flagged on both their EE and their TOK essay. This is not experimentation. It is a pattern of AI-reliant writing across the same student's most important assessments — externally submitted to the IB.

An environment without consequences is an environment that teaches students there are none.
Section 05 — Why This Matters Beyond Detection
What ChatGPT Is Doing to the Writing Brain

Even if every flagged student were caught and addressed, the deeper concern would remain: habitual LLM use during writing tasks appears to be reshaping how students think, retain, and engage. The most striking recent evidence comes from MIT's Media Lab — and it directly concerns the kind of essay writing our students do.

Neural engagement during essay writing — illustrative
MIT Media Lab, 2025

ChatGPT users showed the lowest neural engagement of any group — and couldn't remember what they'd written.

Researchers used EEG to measure brain activity in three groups writing SAT-style essays: one used ChatGPT, one used Google Search, one used no tools. The ChatGPT group showed the weakest connectivity in the alpha and theta bands associated with deep memory processing. When later asked to rewrite an essay without the tool, they recalled very little of their original work. Students who wrote unaided first and used ChatGPT later showed increased connectivity — suggesting AI can support learning when it follows independent thinking, not replace it.

Section 06 — The Gap and the Proposal
Our Current Response Doesn't Work. Here Is What Would.

Our current practice when a student is flagged: if they say they wrote the work and can show Google Docs revision history, we close the case. But Google Docs records keystrokes, not where ideas came from. A student can type AI-generated text while viewing it on another screen, and the edit history looks the same as authentic composition. We are accepting a loophole as evidence of integrity.

⚖ Establish an Academic Integrity Panel
Form a standing panel — teachers, administrators, counselor — to review flagged AI cases with a consistent process
Google Docs history alone is not sufficient as evidence of authentic authorship; panel must require additional verification
Develop a tiered consequence system: first offense, second offense, IB/AP referral thresholds
Maintain confidential institutional records of flagged cases for pattern identification across years
Align consequence policy with IB Academic Integrity policy for cohesion in IB assessments
📋 Classroom-Level Policies
No major writing assignments sent home — all substantial writing completed in supervised class time
When computers are used for writing in class, all screens must be visible to the teacher at all times
Reduce unmonitored computer writing time; increase handwritten drafts, verbal conferencing, and in-class composition
Any AI tool use must be declared on submission with a description of how it was used
Turnitin submission required for all major writing grades; AI score recorded in teacher gradebook
My Proposal — A Unified Response Protocol for All Externally Submitted Papers

Based on two years of data, the amnesty findings, and the external research above. Our current procedures — reflections, supervisor meetings, process journals — have not worked as a deterrent. The protocol below is designed to produce verifiable evidence of authorship, not procedural compliance, and applies to any externally submitted paper regardless of programme.

📋 Proposed Response Protocol — All Externally Submitted Papers (EE · TOK · AP Research · AP Seminar · AP Language)
Any externally submitted paper flagged above 20% AI triggers a mandatory one-hour supervised writing session — same topic, same essay type, no technology access
The supervised sample undergoes linguistic comparison against the flagged submission — vocabulary range, syntactic complexity, argumentation style, and voice consistency are analyzed
Significant divergence between the two samples escalates the case to the Academic Integrity Panel for review
The student has the right to appeal before the panel and present supporting evidence — drafts, notes, research logs, or any documentation of their writing process
If the panel finds against the student: automatic failure of the assessment — no partial credit, no resubmission for the flagged work
Consequences are more severe if a student declines the supervised write, cannot produce process evidence, or is found to have fabricated revision history
Existing programme protocols — IB reflections, supervisor meetings, process journals, AP performance task documentation — remain in place as supporting context but are no longer sufficient as standalone evidence of authorship
Build a portfolio of in-class timed writing samples early in the year to establish a linguistic baseline for each student — essential for meaningful comparison if a paper is later flagged
